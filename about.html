About The Sharkive

A shared, photo-based record of the sharks and rays landed and sold at markets and roadside stalls in Seychelles, and of those seen alive in the sea. It's built so that, over time, the data can show which species are being caught, where, and whether that is changing.

Why it matters

Sharks and rays grow slowly, mature late and have few young, so their populations recover slowly once they decline. Many of the species found in the western Indian Ocean are listed as threatened on the IUCN Red List. Wedgefishes and giant guitarfishes, the "shark-like rays" sometimes sold alongside sharks, are among the most threatened marine fish of all.

Catches from small-scale and artisanal fishing, the sharks and rays you see on a market table or at the roadside, are often not recorded in any detail. Without that information it's hard to know which species are being caught, how big they are, or whether things are getting better or worse.

What we hope the data will do

The aim is to build a long-term record that can, one day, be used to:

  • Show which species are landed, including how often threatened species turn up.
  • Track change over time: in the species mix, in the sizes of animals landed (and how many are juveniles), and across seasons and years.
  • Show how often sharks and rays turn up: checks of markets where there were none for sale are recorded too, so the records can be compared with the times people looked and found nothing.
  • Compare markets with the sea: sightings of live sharks and rays by divers, snorkellers and beach walkers sit alongside the market records.
  • Follow individual animals: species with unique markings, like zebra sharks and eagle rays, can be recognised from photos, so repeat sightings build a life history. See Individuals.
  • Map where sharks are landed and sold around the islands.
  • Support conservation and fisheries management by sharing the data openly. The Records page exports a Darwin Core Archive, the standard format used by GBIF and OBIS, the global biodiversity databases.

Conclusions about populations need a lot of data collected consistently over a long time. Every record adds to that, and the more people who log what they see, the more useful it becomes.

How it works

  1. Photograph each shark or ray you see at a market or roadside seller, or alive in the water or the shallows. If you check a market and there are none for sale, tap Nothing for sale here: that's a useful record too.
  2. When and where are recorded automatically from the photo itself, so every record has a reliable date, time and location. They can't be typed in or changed.
  3. A species suggestion is made on your phone by comparing the photo with photos that have already been confirmed. It is a matching tool, not a generative AI, and it improves as more photos are confirmed.
  4. A reviewer checks every record and confirms the species (and, where the claspers are visible, the sex) before it's added to the database. When an expert or a second reviewer agrees, the record becomes research grade.
  5. Confirmed records are published on the Records page, with a map and a downloadable data file.

Taking a useful photo

  • Show the whole shark from the side if you can, with the fins and tail visible. The shape of the head, the dorsal fins and the tail markings are the main clues to the species. Photograph rays from above.
  • Lay a Sharkive scale card (or a tape, or a sheet of A4) flat beside the animal, so its length can be measured from the photo.
  • If you can, add a photo of the underside behind the pelvic fins: males have two claspers there, females have none.
  • Use the in-app camera, or an original photo from your camera roll. Photos forwarded through WhatsApp or Messenger lose their time and location and can't be used.
  • Enter the number of animals in the photo. If there are several species, log each one separately.
  • In the sea, never chase, touch or crowd a shark or ray for a photo.
  • Frame the shark, not people. Please avoid including faces.
  • Always ask before photographing someone's stall or catch.

This is about sharks, not people

The Sharkive records what is caught and seen. It isn't about who caught or sold it. It doesn't collect the names of fishers or vendors, and it isn't an enforcement tool. Fishers and vendors know more about sharks than almost anyone, and their cooperation is what makes a record like this possible.

Your data

  • No account is needed to log a shark or ray, and no personal details are collected. Your phone is given a random, anonymous ID the first time it uploads, so the app can limit spam. It isn't linked to your name, number or email.
  • Photos stay private until a reviewer approves them. Before anything is uploaded, the app checks on your phone that the photo looks like a shark and isn't inappropriate.
  • Each record stores the photo, when and where it was taken, the place name and details you add, and the type of device used.
  • Published records show the photo, species, date, time and location.

Who runs it

The Sharkive is an independent citizen-science project run from Praslin, Seychelles.

More ways to help sharks and rays in Seychelles

If you'd like to do more than log what you see, the Marine Conservation Society Seychelles (MCSS) is a Seychelles non-profit founded in 1997 that works on marine conservation across the islands. Its work includes research on sharks and rays, sea turtle conservation, coral reef restoration and environmental education. Its website has details of current projects and ways to get involved.
